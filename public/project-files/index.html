# Mercury CRM creative brief

## The idea

Local businesses needed someone to connect their computers in 2002. They face a similar implementation gap today when phones, calendars, and follow-up have to work together. The campaign makes that connection for owners of dental practices, law offices, and local shops and invites them to hear the AI receptionist.

## Visual direction

Hamilton revisited the commercial brief with Muse and supplied a reference ad for pacing and visual language. The Mercury cut opens with a cream mid-century newspaper and period storefronts. A front-facing Bondi Blue iMac cutout, animated network errors, and a technician’s toolbag carry the first act. Each storefront is isolated from the original art and given its own sign and spotlight. At 15.36 seconds the film cuts to Mercury’s navy palette, floating AI badges for phone, calendar, and follow-up, then an illustrative lead pipeline. The badges and the three checkmarks are tied to short chimes. The strategy-call button pulses once near the end. The final beats return to the help-classified theme with the live demo and strategy call.

| Sequence | What the viewer sees |
| --- | --- |
| 0:00–0:08 | Main Street Ledger, the 2002 headline, original storefront artwork, and the iMac network gag |
| 0:08–0:15 | Computer Help classified, toolkit, and circled Call Dave line |
| 0:15–0:23 | Abrupt navy transition, in-phone Mercury logo, and three small-business workflows |
| 0:23–0:30 | Newspaper clipping with a highlighted ratio, five animated storefront icons, and the Census subgroup |
| 0:30–0:43 | Mercury drop-in checklist, a moving pipeline card, and customer relationships |
| 0:43–0:49 | AI demo and strategy-call closing card |

## Captions and usage claims

Short caption phrases drift in and out, with word highlights driven by actual speech timings. The vertical, square-adjacent, and landscape layouts each use dedicated safe areas. Text, artwork, and motion were made for Mercury. The reference ad was used for visual study.

The Census Bureau’s May 2026 analysis covers December 2025 through May 2026. It reports that fewer than 20% of firms with four or fewer employees used AI. The campaign uses that subgroup and the period attribution. [U.S. Census Bureau BTOS analysis](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html)

The offer shown is an AI receptionist and CRM workflow. The campaign omits agency-purchase pricing, an ownership percentage, an operator-hours estimate, and a managed-for-you promise. [Mercury CRM](https://www.getmercurycrm.com/)

# Mercury CRM commercial

## Final narration

Remember 2002? Every dentist, law office, and hardware store needed computers, email, and a network. Before long, every town had a computer guy. A new technology created a new kind of local service.

AI is that moment again. Small businesses need someone to answer calls, book appointments, and follow up. In a 2026 Census report, fewer than one in five firms with four or fewer employees used AI.

Mercury CRM gives you an AI receptionist and one pipeline for calls, bookings, and follow-up. The customer relationship is still yours. The work moves forward, even when you're busy.

Hear it for yourself. Try the AI demo, or book a strategy call.

# The 18% Problem: Running AI Like a Company, Not a Chatbot

A conversational audio adaptation of Hamilton Pinto Jr.’s MasterClass Executive thesis. The host and guest are synthetic narrators. The figures and operating model are attributed to the [original thesis](https://hamiltonpinto.com/masterclass/the-18-percent-problem-running-ai-like-a-company-not-a-chatbot).

**Host**

Welcome. Today we’re talking about The 18% Problem: Running AI Like a Company, Not a Chatbot, a MasterClass Executive thesis by Hamilton Pinto Junior. I keep coming back to that title. What actually changes when you give software a job to do?

**Guest**

Think about the difference between writing a reply and sending one. The first is a draft. The second reaches someone, makes a commitment, and leaves your company responsible for it. Once agents can act on your behalf, management starts to matter as much as model quality. You need to know which agents exist, what access they have, and who is accountable for the outcome.

**Host**

And that’s where the 18% comes in, right? The thesis cites IBM Think 2026 research that found only 18% of organizations kept a complete inventory of their AI agents. What does that look like on an ordinary workday?

**Guest**

Imagine that a customer gets a message they weren’t supposed to receive. If your team can’t tell which agent sent it or which permissions it used, you’re investigating from scratch. Hamilton describes giving each agent a named role, a clear brief, and scoped access. An agent can prepare the message. The authority to send it can go through a separate human approval. It’s a management decision you can apply to the next workflow you build.

**Host**

Okay, so first I can answer who works here and what they’re allowed to do. Hamilton also uses the phrase verifiable chain of custody. What would I actually be able to see?

**Guest**

Let’s say an agent books an appointment. A reviewer should be able to see four things. Which agent requested it, who or what approved it, the action it took, and the result that came back. Link those entries in a tamper-evident record and you can spot if someone changes the history later. That’s Hamilton’s proposal for agent operations. The inventory research tells us how far organizations have to go on the basics. The chain of custody is his argument for what comes next.

**Host**

If listeners want to try this with one agent this week, where would you have them start?

**Guest**

Pick one action your customers can see. Ask someone on your team to show you what the agent is allowed to do, who approved its last action, and where the result is recorded. Any question they can’t answer is a concrete place to start.

**Host**

I like that. One workflow, four things you can trace. Thanks for joining us and making this so practical. And thanks to everyone listening. The full thesis is right here on the site if you’d like to dig into it. See you next time.

# AI Lab 04 walkthrough — Hamilton Pinto Jr.

Record in your own voice with screen capture. Aim for about four minutes. Show the campaign and its final export, and demonstrate the thesis podcast as a companion project. Keep the audio from the clips short so your own narration remains the focus.

## 0:00–0:45 · The problem and audience

> Screen cue — Open the Lab 04 showcase. Show the Mercury CRM video and podcast side by side in the page navigation.

I’m Hamilton Pinto Junior. For this lab I wanted to explain two kinds of change that are easy to make sound abstract. The first is the opportunity for someone who wants to run a local AI services business. The second is the responsibility that comes with giving AI agents something real to do.

Mercury CRM’s audience is entrepreneurs who can work with local businesses but don’t want to build all the technical infrastructure themselves. I wanted a short commercial that makes that opportunity concrete. I also wanted people visiting my thesis to have an easy way to listen, so I made a podcast version.

## 0:45–1:50 · The 18% Problem podcast

> Screen cue — Open the thesis, show the inline player and transcript, and play a short host-to-guest exchange. Return to the project and show the same episode.

My thesis is called The 18% Problem: Running AI Like a Company, Not a Chatbot. The number comes from research cited in the article. Only eighteen percent of organizations surveyed kept a complete inventory of their AI agents. To me, that sounds like a management problem. If an agent can send a customer message or schedule something, a human needs to know which agent did it and what gave it permission.

I adapted the thesis into a conversation. The host asks the practical questions, and the guest explains the named roles, limited access, and approval steps. I wanted listeners to hear what a chain of custody means in ordinary work. An agent requests an action, someone approves it, the action runs, and we can see the result. The audio and transcript are available on the thesis and here in the lab.

## 1:50–3:20 · Mercury CRM commercial

> Screen cue — Show your ElevenCreative Flow, the original script, the voice choice, the visual and music stages, then play the finished campaign. Show the social exports and the closing screen.

For the commercial I started with a memory. When local businesses first needed networks and email, somebody in town knew how to set them up. I used that analogy to make the role of an AI receptionist clear. Businesses now need help answering calls, booking appointments, and following up. The commercial shows how Mercury connects the receptionist, appointment booking, and follow-up in one pipeline.

I used ElevenLabs through MCP to work on the voice and campaign stages. I directed the commercial voice separately from the two podcast roles. I used a mid-century newspaper and Main Street imagery for the history. The campaign then cuts to navy, and word-timed captions carry the narration through the Mercury product flow. I gave the closing screen one action. Book a strategy call. I also prepared the campaign for vertical, horizontal, and portrait feeds, because the same type and crop don’t work everywhere.

## 3:20–4:05 · Judgment and handoff

> Screen cue — Show the production credits and downloads. End on the campaign CTA or the project page.

I’ll judge this by whether someone understands the offer and knows what to do next after they watch. For the thesis, the goal is simpler. A visitor should be able to press play and follow an idea about running AI agents while they’re doing something else.

I used Gemini to shape the specification, Muse to revisit the commercial brief, and Codex with ChatGPT 6.2 Sol on high to build the project. Through ElevenLabs MCP I produced the voices, music, art, and caption timing. I’m recording this walkthrough in my own voice. For the edit I’m using Claude on Otto3, my production server, where multiple agents can support the edit at the same time. The lab page has the assets and the record of what went into them.
